Identify the Spanish laughing-interview meme described by the user, verify the source, and create two original English subtitle parodies about agentic software engineering producing work faster than humans and CI can absorb it. Keep the original Spanish speech and laughter. The subtitles are fictional parody, not a translation.

## Creative direction
- The joke targets a process bottleneck: code production scales faster than review, confidence, and infrastructure.
- The storyteller is the engineer delighted by absurd productivity metrics; the interviewer supplies short prompts.
- Escalation: small productivity win → reviewer overload → recursive AI review → CI queue → absurd management success metric.
- Sisyphus is a possible visual/verbal callback: every cleared queue reveals a larger queue.
- Keep technical jokes understandable; use concrete numbers as fictional exaggeration, not measured claims.
- Preserve the performance and laughter; no voice cloning, dubbed impersonation, or random ordering of scenes.

## Editing and acceptance criteria
- Aim for approximately 60–90 seconds per version, adjusted to the source performance.
- Use no more than two subtitle lines; readable high-contrast text with an outline; avoid captioning every laugh.
- Hold each punchline through the first laugh, then give the laugh room to breathe.
- Preserve original framing for a landscape master; use padded/reframed vertical delivery only if both faces and captions remain readable.
- Export H.264 MP4 with AAC audio and fast-start metadata. Match source frame rate; avoid inventing resolution detail through upscaling.
- Watch each export end-to-end to check synchronization, caption clipping, audio, and the ending.
- Mark captions as parody and identify the original performer/show in the accompanying description. Check source permissions/platform requirements before public posting; finding an upload does not establish reuse rights.

## Script A — Merge Hell: They thought they were catching up

Full subtitle draft, revised with “ten more” and the split into three pull requests. Speaker labels and bracketed directions are editing notes, not burned-in captions. Line breaks suggest phrasing; final caption cards and timing must follow the footage. These expanded scripts supersede the earlier short beat tables. Allow a longer cut if needed rather than forcing them into the initial 60–90-second target.

```text
HOST:
So, agentic coding made you faster?

GUEST:
Incredible.
A whole week's code before breakfast.

Forty pull requests.
I tagged the team.

The lead said,
“We need time to review these properly.”

Of course! Absolutely!
Quality comes first.

So while they reviewed them…

I made ten more.

[Erupts laughing]

Ten more!

And they were doing good reviews!
Checking edge cases. Finding real bugs.

One guy spent two hours
understanding a race condition.

During those two hours,
I'd created three new services.

He was still on the first service!

[Laugh — leave space]

Then they organized a review day.
“No coding. Everyone clears the backlog.”

Everyone except my agents.

By lunch they'd reviewed fifty.
The queue had grown by ninety.

And they said,
“We're making good progress.”

Good progress!

HOST:
Couldn't you help them?

GUEST:
I did!
I built a dashboard to track the backlog.

Then I tagged them to review it.

[Laugh]

They asked for smaller pull requests.

Very reasonable.
Much easier to understand.

So I split the next one into three.

Three pull requests!
Three reviews!
Three pipelines!

Then the lead said,
“This is like Sisyphus.”

I said, “No, he had one rock.”

Our rocks create more rocks.

[Big laugh]

At five they said,
“We'll finish the rest tomorrow.”

Tomorrow!

I left the agents running overnight.

They'll be reviewing
until Tomorrowland.

And management asked
if we'd saved any time.

I said, “I've saved loads.”

Theirs?
I've booked the next six years.
```

Direction: place “Ten more!”, the three-part pull-request callback, and “Tomorrow!” around suitable laugh beats. Preserve the genuine effort and competence of the reviewers: the joke is that incoming work outpaces their capacity. Let the dashboard joke land before escalating to the Sisyphus and overnight callbacks.

## Script B — We Automated the Review: Ninety-second tests

Full subtitle draft. Speaker labels and bracketed directions are editing notes. Timing remains provisional until footage inspection.

```text
HOST:
What did you do about the review bottleneck?

GUEST:
We added an AI reviewer.
To save the developers time.

First pull request:
three hundred helpful suggestions.

And some were really good!
So the coding agent fixed them.

Three hundred fixes.

Then the reviewer reviewed the fixes.

[Starts laughing]

More suggestions!

One agent renamed a function.
The other wanted a clearer name.

They discussed it all morning.

Through commits!

Every commit started a pipeline.

HOST:
But your tests run quickly?

GUEST:
Ninety seconds.
We spent months optimizing them.

Ninety seconds!

After six hours
waiting for a runner.

[Big laugh]

At nine, all the developers logged in.
Each one started five agents.

The runners were fully booked
before anyone finished saying “stand-up.”

So we doubled the runners.

The queue started moving!
The agents got feedback faster!

And immediately submitted more fixes.

We'd upgraded the traffic jam.

[Laugh]

Then we added an autoscaler.

Finally, something
that could keep up with the agents.

The cloud bill.

HOST:
And the human reviewers?

GUEST:
Still there!

Someone has to check
whether the AI's suggestion makes sense.

Now they read the code,
the review, the fix…

and the review of the fix.

We automated everything
except understanding what we'd done.

[Pause, then laugh]

Finance asked us
to reduce infrastructure costs.

No problem.
The agent wrote a patch.

Beautiful patch.
Turns off unnecessary runners.

HOST:
Did you deploy it?

GUEST:
Not yet.

It's waiting for a runner.

[Hold through the laugh]

And the AI reviewer
has a few suggestions.
```

Direction: give “The cloud bill” and “It's waiting for a runner” separate subtitle cards. Deliver “Ninety seconds!” proudly and reveal the queue delay afterward. Preserve the distinction between useful AI feedback and the extra human verification and infrastructure work it can generate. If a host turn cannot be matched to the footage, adapt it into the guest quoting a question rather than captioning a silent host as speaking.

## Recommendation and production specification

Start with Script A: its premise is clearer, its escalation stays focused, and “the dashboard needs review” is a strong intermediate joke. Release Script B separately as a follow-up rather than squeezing both premises into one clip.

Use the original Spanish audio without dubbing. Mark the opening or description “Parody subtitles — not a translation.” Avoid added laugh tracks and music under the performance. Retain room for the guest to recover between jokes. Keep the host's skeptical reactions wherever the source offers them; do not invent a visible speaking turn on a silent reaction shot.

For caption layout, aim for 32–40 characters per line and no more than two lines. Break on sense units; aim for approximately 12–17 characters per second, with punchlines held longer when the laughter permits. Use white semibold sans-serif with a black outline, inside a generous bottom safe margin. Confirm actual readability on a phone. Do not cover existing subtitles with unreadable stacked text; acquire clean footage or use a clearly designed subtitle band.

Create a landscape master preserving the source aspect ratio. For a vertical version, fit the interview inside a 1080×1920 canvas with a short title above and subtitles below rather than cropping away the interviewer. Source resolution may be much lower; padding a delivery canvas does not add detail.

Export H.264 video, yuv420p, AAC audio, original frame rate, and MP4 fast-start. Deliver burned-in-caption MP4s plus SRT/ASS files separately. Keep edit decisions in a source-timecode table. Do not use the transcript anchors above as final SRT timestamps.
